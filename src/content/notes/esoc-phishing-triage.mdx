If there's one alert type every SOC analyst gets good at whether they like it or not, it's phishing. It's also one where speed matters more than usual: every minute you spend deciding is a minute someone else might be clicking the same link.

This is the routine I'd follow. The example email is made up; the domains use the reserved `.example` suffix so nothing here points anywhere real.

## 1. Read the report, then the email

Start with what the user said. "I clicked it" and "it looked odd so I reported it" are very different incidents, and the first one changes the priority immediately.

Then look at the message itself, the way the user saw it: sender name, subject, the ask. Most phishing wants one of three things: credentials, a payment, or a file opened.

## 2. Headers tell you where it really came from

The display name is decoration. The headers are evidence. Here's a trimmed example:

```text
Return-Path: <bounce@mailer.billing.example>
From: "Accounts Payable" <ap@contoso-payments.example>
Reply-To: ap.desk@freemail.example
Subject: Overdue invoice INV-20931
Authentication-Results: mx.example.com;
       spf=pass smtp.mailfrom=mailer.billing.example;
       dkim=pass header.d=mailer.billing.example;
       dmarc=fail (p=none) header.from=contoso-payments.example
```

What I'd notice, in order:

- **`Reply-To` is a free mail address.** Replies go somewhere the "company" doesn't control. Classic for invoice and payment fraud.
- **SPF and DKIM pass, but for a different domain.** They prove `mailer.billing.example` sent it, not `contoso-payments.example`. That's why DMARC fails: the domains don't line up.
- **`p=none`.** The sending domain's DMARC policy is only monitoring, so the failure didn't stop delivery.

Read the `Received:` lines from the bottom up. The lowest one is closest to the real origin.

## 3. Links: look, don't click

- **Defang before anything else.** `hxxps://login-billing[.]example/reset` can be pasted into a ticket safely.
- **Hover-check the real target.** The text says one domain; the `href` often says another.
- **Expand shortened links** with a URL expander or a sandbox, not your browser.
- **Look at the landing page in a sandbox** if you need to. A fake Microsoft 365 login page is a credential-harvesting confirmation all on its own.

## 4. Attachments: hash first

Get the file's hash and look it up before doing anything else. Uploading the file to a public service can expose whatever's inside it.

```powershell
# On an analysis machine, never on the user's laptop
Get-FileHash .\INV-20931.html -Algorithm SHA256
```

```bash
sha256sum INV-20931.html
```

If the hash is unknown and you need more, detonate it in a sandbox your organisation approves. HTML attachments, `.iso`, `.zip` with scripts inside, and Office files with macros are the usual suspects.

## 5. Scope it: who else got this?

One report usually means many recipients. In Defender, `EmailEvents` answers who received it:

```kql
EmailEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(7d)
| where SenderFromAddress =~ "ap@contoso-payments.example"
    or Subject has "INV-20931"
| project Timestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, SenderFromAddress, Subject,
          DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation, NetworkMessageId
```

And `UrlClickEvents` answers the question that actually matters, who clicked:

```kql
UrlClickEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(7d)
| where Url has "login-billing.example"
| project Timestamp, AccountUpn, Url, ActionType, IsClickedThrough
```

If someone clicked and the page was a credential harvester, check their sign-ins straight after:

```kql
SigninLogs
| where TimeGenerated > ago(2d)
| where UserPrincipalName =~ "user@yourcompany.example"
| project TimeGenerated, IPAddress, Location = tostring(LocationDetails.countryOrRegion),
          AppDisplayName, ResultType, UserAgent
| order by TimeGenerated desc
```

A successful sign-in from a new country or an unusual user agent shortly after the click turns this into an account compromise.

## 6. Respond

Depending on what you found, and what your playbook allows at your level:

- **Remove the email** from every mailbox that received it (in Defender, Threat Explorer can soft-delete it across the tenant).
- **Block the sender, domain and URLs** in the Tenant Allow/Block List or your mail gateway.
- **If credentials were entered:** reset the password, revoke sessions and check MFA methods for anything newly added.

```powershell
# Microsoft Graph PowerShell: sign the user out everywhere
Revoke-MgUserSignInSession -UserId "user@yourcompany.example"
```

- **Look for persistence in the mailbox:** new inbox rules that forward or hide mail are a common follow-up.
- **Tell the reporter what happened.** People who report phishing and never hear back stop reporting.

## 7. Close it properly

The case notes should answer, without anyone having to ask: what was it, who got it, who clicked, who entered anything, what was removed or blocked, and what's still open. Add the indicators (sender, domains, URLs, hashes) in a defanged list so the next analyst can search for them.

And if the same campaign keeps coming back, that's a detection or mail-filter improvement to suggest, not just another ticket to close.
